Block 1 · The big picture

AI, ML and deep learning: circles inside circles

Artificial intelligenceany technique that makes computers act smart: rules, search, planning, learning
Machine learninglearns rules from examples instead of being told them
Deep learningML with many-layer neural networks: images, speech, LLMs

Why learn rules instead of writing them?

Traditional programming

data + rules you write → answers

"if contract is month-to-month and bill > $70, flag the customer"

Machine learning

data + answers from the past → rules the computer finds

7,043 past customers + who left → a model that scores new customers

Why? ML wins when the rules are too many, too subtle, or keep changing. It loses when a simple rule already works, or when you have no past answers to learn from.

Block 2 · Framing

Turn a business wish into a prediction task

1 Business wish: "we lose too many customers"
2 Prediction question: which current customers will cancel in the next month?
3 Target (y): Churn = Yes / No  ·  Features (X): contract, tenure, bill, services…
4 Decision: the retention team calls the riskiest customers first

The problem-statement template (your first deliverable)

We want to predict [target] for [each row = who/what] using [features available at prediction time], so that [who] can [decision]. We will measure success with [metric] and must beat [baseline].
Why? The phrase "available at prediction time" is where most leakage is caught. If you would not know a column's value on the day you predict, it cannot be a feature.

What kind of task is it?

TaskTarget looks likeExample from our datasetsLearning type
Regressiona numbermedical charges in $, house pricesupervised
Classificationa category (often yes/no)churn yes/no, income > $50K, survivedsupervised
Clusteringno target at allgroup customers into segmentsunsupervised
Anomaly detectionno target (mostly)flag strange transactionsunsupervised

Supervised vs unsupervised

Supervised

every training row has the answer (label)
→ learn to predict it for new rows

like studying with an answer key

Unsupervised

no answers; find structure: groups, unusual rows, simpler summaries

like sorting a pile of photos into piles without being told the categories

Why? Labels are often the expensive part: someone must wait a month to see who churned, or pay a doctor to read every scan. That cost decides what is possible.

First check: how balanced is the target?

Why? Only 26.5% of customers left. A model that always says "stays" is right 73.5% of the time while being completely useless. Know this number before you trust any accuracy.

Why we split

A student who saw the exam paper the night before

Why? …scores 100%, but has he learned? Testing a model on data it trained on is the same cheat. We hide some rows (the test set) and only use them to grade the model at the very end.

Train, validation, test: three jobs

Train (~60–80%)

the model learns from these rows

Validation

we choose between models and settings (or use cross-validation instead)

Test (~20%)

a locked box: opened once, at the end, for the final honest score

Why? If you look at the test score, change something, and look again, the test set has become a validation set, and your final score is optimistic.

One split is luck: same model, 20 random splits

Why? Same data, same model: 78.8% to 81.8% depending only on which customers landed in the test set. That is why we fix random_state (repeatable) and use cross-validation for decisions (Session 2: 80.2% ± 1.2%).

How to split: all the options

MethodHowUse when
Randomshuffle, cut 80/20regression; rows are independent
Stratifiedrandom, but keep the class balance equal in both partsclassification, especially imbalanced
Time-basedtrain on the past, test on the most recent periodanything you will predict in the future: sales, fraud, churn over months
Group-basedall rows of one customer/patient go to the same sideseveral rows per person (visits, transactions)
Best choice: stratified for classification, random for regression, time-based whenever the data has dates, group-based when one person has many rows. Our churn file is one snapshot, one row per customer → stratified.

The most expensive mistake in ML

Leakage: the answer sneaks into the question

Why? Leakage = the model gets information during training that it will not have when used for real. The score looks amazing; the model fails in production. And nobody notices until real money is lost.

Two leaks, measured on our churn data

Why? Left: a column recorded after customers cancel ("days since last payment"; simulated) jumps accuracy to 93.4%. On the day we predict, nobody has cancelled yet, so it's useless. Right: rows copied twice end up on both sides of the split; the tree recognises "test" customers it already memorised.

The five leaks to hunt for

LeakExamplePrevention
Target leakagea column created after the outcome: "cancellation reason", "final bill"ask: is it known at prediction time?
Preprocessing before splitscaler/imputer fitted on all rows, so test statistics leak into trainingsplit first; put preprocessing in a pipeline
Duplicatesthe same record on both sides of the splitcheck df.duplicated() before splitting
Time traveltraining on next month to predict last monthtime-based split
Group leakageone patient's scans in both train and testgroup-based split
Why? Red flag: a score that seems too good to be true usually is. Find the column doing the work before celebrating.

Block 4 · Preprocessing

Numbers: fill the gaps, put them on one scale

Missing values

models can't do maths on blanks
→ fill with the median (robust to outliers)
→ but first ask why it's missing: our 11 blank TotalCharges are brand-new customers → $0 is the truth

Scaling

TotalCharges runs to 8,000; tenure to 72
→ subtract the mean, divide by the spread (StandardScaler)
→ needed for linear models, distance methods, neural nets; trees don't care

Categories: turning words into numbers

OptionContract → numbersWhen
Label numbersmonth=0, one-year=1, two-year=2❌ for unordered categories (invents a false order)
One-hotthree 0/1 columns, one per category✅ few categories, no natural order
Ordinalkeep a real order: small < medium < large✅ categories with a true order
Target / frequency encodingreplace a category with a statistichundreds of categories (cities); careful: leaks if done before the split
Best choice: one-hot by default (OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore") so a new category in production doesn't crash the system); ordinal only when the order is real.

The golden rule of preprocessing

1 fit = learn something from data (the mean, the median, the list of categories)
2 Fit only on training rows
3 transform train, test and future rows with what was learned
Why? If the scaler learns the mean from all rows, the test set has already "influenced" training: a small leak. In real life, tomorrow's customers can't influence today's training either.

Block 5 · Baselines

Before any model: what does "no intelligence" score?

Why? A baseline is the simplest possible answer: "always predict the most common class" or "always predict the average", or a one-line business rule. Without it, "80% accuracy" means nothing.

Baselines vs a real model on our churn test set

What the baselines teach us

1 Accuracy alone lies: the "always stays" model scores 73.5% and catches no one
2 A one-line rule catches 88% of leavers but wastes most calls (precision 43%)
3 The learned model is better balanced, but beats the sharper rule by only ~5 points of accuracy
Why? If a simple rule is "good enough" for the business, ship the rule: cheaper, explainable, nothing to maintain. A model must earn its complexity against the best baseline.

Block 6 · scikit-learn

Every tool speaks the same language

ObjectMethodsExamples
Transformerfit(X) → learn · transform(X) → change dataSimpleImputer, StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder
Estimator / modelfit(X, y) → learn · predict(X) → answersDummyClassifier, LogisticRegression, RandomForest
Pipelinechains them; behaves like one modelimpute → scale → encode → model
Why? Same interface everywhere: once you know fit / transform / predict, you can swap any model in one line.

The pipeline: preprocessing + model as one object

raw rows
numbers: impute median → scale
categories: impute → one-hot
model
prediction
1 No leakage: fitting the pipeline fits every step on training rows only
2 No mismatch: new rows get exactly the same treatment
3 One file to save, test and deploy (Session 4)

The whole of today in code

X = df.drop(columns=["customerID", "Churn"])          # features
y = (df["Churn"] == "Yes").astype(int)                # target

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(  # split FIRST
    X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=42)

pre = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median"), StandardScaler()), numeric_cols),
    ("cat", make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="most_frequent"),
                          OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore")), categorical_cols)])

baseline = make_pipeline(pre, DummyClassifier(strategy="most_frequent"))
baseline.fit(X_train, y_train)                        # learns from train only
print(accuracy_score(y_test, baseline.predict(X_test)))   # 0.7346

Check yourself · 1 of 5

A model scores 95% accuracy predicting a disease that 5% of patients have. Impressive?

AnswerNot yet. "Always healthy" also scores 95%. Check the baseline and recall first.

Check yourself · 2 of 5

A column "refund_issued" predicts churn almost perfectly. Use it?

AnswerNo. Refunds happen after cancelling; it's not known at prediction time: target leakage.

Check yourself · 3 of 5

You fit StandardScaler on the whole dataset, then split. What's wrong?

AnswerTest-set statistics influenced training: preprocessing before split. Split first; use a pipeline.

Check yourself · 4 of 5

You predict next month's sales from 3 years of daily data. Which split?

AnswerTime-based: train on older dates, test on the most recent period.
