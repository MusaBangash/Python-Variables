The two mistakes do not cost the same

False alarm (110)

We call a happy customer. Cost: one phone call, about $20.

Miss (162)

A leaver we never called. Cost: their bills, about $390 (6 months × $64).

Why? Accuracy treats a $20 mistake and a $390 mistake as equal. The business never does.

Two better questions

Precision

Of the customers we call, how many were really leaving?

65.8%

212 ÷ (212 + 110)

Recall

Of all customers who left, how many did we catch?

56.7%

212 ÷ (212 + 162)

One number for both

F1 score = 60.9%

Why? F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall: it is high only when both are high. Use it when you have no cost numbers. When you know the costs, use them instead (coming up).

The hidden setting

The model gives a risk score. We choose where to cut.

Why? "Call if risk ≥ 0.5" is just a default. Move the cut and precision and recall move in opposite directions.

Why? In the test set, the 200-call budget scales to 40 calls; 30 of the model's top 40 were real leavers. Same effort, nearly 3× the hits of random calling.

If calls are not capped: let the money decide the cut

Call if  risk × 30% × $390  >  $20

→ call anyone with risk above 0.17

Why? Expected gain of a call = chance they leave × chance our offer works × what they are worth. Call when it beats the call's cost. Assumptions: 30% of offers succeed, a saved customer is worth $390, a call costs $20.

Best choice here: keep the plain model and move the threshold. Weighting gave the same ranking quality (AUC 0.841 vs 0.842), so it only moved the cut, and it broke the probabilities (next slide).

Honest probabilities: calibration

Why? The plain model is honest: when it says 35%, 35% leave. The "balanced" model says 59% when only 33% leave. Feed that into the profit formula and every money decision is wrong.

More than two classes?

Why? Most modern models handle many classes directly (softmax, Lesson 4). These strategies matter for older binary-only models and for reporting.

Check yourself · 1 of 5

Accuracy 95% on fraud data where 2% is fraud. Impressed?

AnswerNot yet: predicting "never fraud" scores 98%. Check recall and precision on the fraud class.

Check yourself · 2 of 5

Precision vs recall: which matters more for cancer screening?

AnswerRecall: missing a cancer is far worse than a follow-up test on a healthy person.

Check yourself · 3 of 5

Why is 0.5 not automatically the right threshold?

AnswerIt ignores costs and capacity. Choose the cut from the cost of each mistake or the number of actions you can take.

Check yourself · 4 of 5

Two models: AUC 0.84 and 0.84, but one is badly calibrated. Does it matter?

AnswerYes, if scores feed money decisions: a badly calibrated model over- or under-states risk, so the profit maths goes wrong.

Check yourself · 5 of 5

Your model misses long-time customers who leave. What do you try first?

AnswerNew features that capture change (bill increases, complaints), before trying a more complex model.
